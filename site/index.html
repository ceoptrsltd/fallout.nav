01 What it does

One small box, nine jobs

A Raspberry Pi Compute Module 4 behind the dashboard of a camper van records the road ahead and behind, knows where it is and which way it points, guards the van while you're away, and draws all of it on a tube older than most smartphones.

Two‑camera dashcam

Front: the whole 2592×1944 sensor, scaled (never cropped) to 1440×1080 H.264 at 15 fps. Rear: a 2560×1440 Wi‑Fi camera, recorded as is, with its microphone. Ten‑minute clips survive a yanked power cable with at most two seconds lost, each with its GPS track, and a hard knock while driving protects both cameras' clips.

  • MPEG‑TS
  • fsync 1 s
  • 10 GB loop
  • seek anywhere
  • GPX

Moving satellite map

A hand‑written Canvas renderer for Esri World Imagery and streets. In heading‑up mode the car icon turns first and the map catches up on a spring. Zoom follows speed; when you stop, the screen switches to the camera; in a tunnel the map keeps going on dead reckoning.

  • Esri tiles
  • 1 GB cache
  • zoom 15–19
  • heading/north‑up

Wasteland HUD

Three compass designs, tall Oswald digits, stencil warnings and a spiked muscle car, all sized for a 92 × 69 mm picture. A clock, the weather for now and the next two days with wind streaks around the car, a speed‑limit sign from offline OpenStreetMap roads, and a car‑radio tuner panel.

  • v1 · v2 · v3
  • Open‑Meteo
  • OSM limits
  • burn‑in shift

Sensors that tune themselves

A Holybro M8N set up over UBX for 10 Hz fixes, an IST8310 compass that calibrates itself against the GPS course while you drive, and an MPU‑6050 gyro that carries the heading through turns, tilt‑compensates the compass and notices reverse gear, so the rear camera comes up on its own.

  • UBX 10 Hz
  • auto‑calibration
  • gyro fusion
  • gpsd + chrony

Van alarm

Knocks, tilt, the van moving, a camera going dark, and people lingering at the van, spotted by a neural network on both cameras. It answers with the hazard lights, a siren on the speaker and protected recordings, and sends a push to your phone with a snapshot from five seconds before.

  • MPU‑6050
  • NanoDet · NCNN
  • Web Push
  • more ↓

A level for the campsite

Park and the speedometer turns into a level: pitch and roll from the MPU‑6050, calibrated once against a spirit level, and how many centimetres to raise each wheel, drawn with our own alcove camper on a 2001 Peugeot Boxer.

  • pitch · roll
  • cm per wheel
  • LEVEL ≤ 0.3°

Radio and games

Internet radio from radio‑browser.info by mood, genre or era on a Bluetooth speaker, driven by a six‑key macro pad with a volume knob like a car radio. Parked, a gamepad opens a games menu on the tube: Freedoom, a Game Boy homebrew, the Duke Nukem 3D shareware episode.

  • mpv
  • bluez‑alsa
  • favorites
  • RetroArch
  • EDuke32

Web control room, anywhere

A password‑protected web app for the phone or laptop, in the van or from anywhere through an ssh tunnel to your own server: live video and rear‑camera sound, recordings with event markers, the alarm, a log of every place the van has stopped, read‑only guest links and a 1:1 preview of the tube.

  • FastAPI
  • installable app
  • WebRTC
  • Tracking

Composite, straight to the tube

No HDMI converter. One wire soldered to pin 111 (VDAC_COMP) of the CM4 goes into the monitor's DIN socket. NTSC 480i/60, with margins and mirroring tuned to the RV‑50, and a pixel or two of drift every few minutes so nothing burns in.

  • pin 111
  • NTSC 480i
  • mirror_x
  • KMS margins

02 Guard mode

Walk away, keep watch

Press Arm in the web app and walk off: a minute later the van is on guard. The tube goes almost black, with a dim "ALARM ON" and the clock, while the sensors and both cameras keep looking.

  • 0.15 gA knock on the bodyThe MPU‑6050 samples at 200 Hz. The threshold is set right on a live vibration meter, above what wind and passing trucks give.
  • 3°A tilt that staysHeld for more than five seconds: a jack under the van, or a tow truck.
  • 100 mThe van movesFive good GPS fixes in a row over ten seconds, so one GPS jump never sounds the siren.
  • 20 sA camera goes darkEither camera stops sending video while armed.
  • 2–8 sSomeone at the vanNanoDet‑Plus through NCNN on both cameras: a person coming close, a face at the windscreen, someone lingering. Only people count; anything else is just a mark on the timeline.

Answers

Hazard lights through a relay wired across the hazard button, a 30‑second siren on the Bluetooth speaker (never for camera‑only events), and both cameras' current clips protected from the loop.

Tells you

A Web Push to Android or iPhone with a snapshot from five seconds before the event and a Silence button. Every event lands in a log with its snapshot and a Play button that jumps into the recording. If the van drops offline, you hear about that too.

Keeps going

Arming survives a restart, a full disk and a read‑only file system. Events wait in an outbox until the server is reachable, and the push relay runs on your own server, not in someone else's cloud.

03 In the van

Bolted to the dashboard

The RV‑50 sits on the dashboard of a 2001 Peugeot Boxer alcove camper, where a reversing monitor belongs. The Pi, front camera, GPS and motion sensor live behind it on a single 12 V feed; the rear camera rides on the back and joins over Wi‑Fi.

  • 92×69Millimetres of pictureThe visible area of the 5″ tube. Every font, the compass and the car were sized for it in the 1:1 preview.
  • 10 GBOf looped videoThe default cap for both cameras on the 32 GB eMMC. The oldest clips go first, protected ones never, and protected clips can't squeeze the loop below an hour.
  • 1 GBOf map tiles cachedTiles come through the phone hotspot. Roads you have already driven work offline.
  • 0Converters in the video pathComposite leaves the CM4 on a single wire and goes straight into the monitor's channel‑2 input.
  • 6Keys and a knobA USB macro pad by the wheel: previous and next station, map or camera, front or rear, protect the clip, radio mode; the knob is the volume.

Video: pin 111 → DIN camera input

The CM4's composite DAC is on the module connector but the carrier board doesn't route it out, so it gets a wire of its own.

The 40‑pin header: GPS, motion sensor, hazard relay

RX/TX are labelled on the module side, so they cross over. The GPS gets the PL011 UART5 on pins 32/33, leaving the main UART to the on‑board Bluetooth that plays the radio. Everything is 3.3 V logic and connects directly.

The IST8310 compass answers on I²C‑1 at 0x0E; the MPU‑6050 has I²C‑3 to itself at 0x68. The GPS button: a short press flips the car screen between the front and rear camera, a two‑second press protects both cameras' clips, and its LED blinks while recording.

05 Architecture

Ten services, no single point of failure

Each camera records in its own process: if the web server falls over, both dashcams keep rolling. Person detection, the radio and the games run as services of their own, systemd restarts anything that dies, and the display is just a web page in a kiosk browser. From outside, the van is reached through an ssh tunnel to your own server, which also keeps the position log and sends the push notifications.

06 The hard parts

Lessons from the wasteland

Most of it went to plan. These parts didn't, and they're the reason the device works.

01

A recorder that survives the ignition key

Car power dies without warning. An MP4 keeps its index at the end of the file, so if the power goes mid‑clip the whole file is unreadable. fallout.nav writes MPEG‑TS instead: independent 188‑byte packets with no index, playable up to the last byte written.

1 s2 s3 s4 s5 s6 s7 s8 scut

The recorder calls fsync() every second, places a keyframe every second, and on boot trims the last file to a whole number of packets. Pull the plug and you lose at most two seconds, not ten minutes.

LessonPick formats that fail gracefully, then make the OS flush on your schedule.

02

Pin 111, not pin 109

The CM4 has a composite video DAC, but the Waveshare carrier doesn't route it to a connector. So a wire goes straight onto pin 111 (VDAC_COMP) of the module's J2 connector. Counting pins at that pitch goes wrong easily; the grounds make a better landmark. On the inner row a ground comes after every two signal pins:

101103105107 ⏚109111113 ⏚115

The right pin is the left one of the pair between the first two grounds. One pin to the left is 109, PCIe_nRST: a flat 0 V and an hour of head‑scratching.

LessonNavigate by landmarks, not by counting.

03

PAL jumped, NTSC held

The plan said PAL 576i/50. The 2001 tube couldn't hold vertical sync: the frame jumped with 288p, mono, firmware or KMS output, dimming and DC‑level shifts alike. At NTSC 480i/60 it locks solid. At 60 Hz the monitor only shows the central ~80 % × 90 %, so kernel margins squeeze the picture into the visible area.

LessonOld hardware has opinions. Try the boring alternative early.

04

The monitor mirrors everything

The RV‑50 is a reversing monitor: it flips the picture so the rear view reads like a mirror. The first map came out backwards. Now the display renders mirrored (mirror_x), and even the 12 px centring shift goes the "wrong" way: right on the screen is left in the signal.

LessonRead the device's purpose, not just its spec sheet.

05

Two inputs, one ghost

The monitor isolates its camera inputs poorly. The van's own reversing camera on the neighbouring channel bleeds into ours, and ours into it. You can't fix that with a soldering iron inside a 25‑year‑old monitor, so the fix is in software: a control that blanks fallout.nav's output, so the reversing camera gets a clean picture when it matters.

Ground matters too: Pi, DC‑DC and monitor share one ground, otherwise the DC‑DC converter's noise shows up as rolling bars.

LessonWhen the hardware leaks, give the user an off switch.

06

A compass that calibrates itself

Inside a steel van, a magnetometer mostly measures the van. Instead of a figure‑of‑eight dance in a car park, fnav‑core collects samples while you drive straight above 5 m/s, compares them with the GPS course, and fits hard‑iron offsets and a deviation curve. Drive in different directions and the coverage ring fills up. While moving, the heading follows the GPS course with the gyro carrying it through turns; when you stop, the compass takes over from the last GPS course, so the arrow doesn't jump.

LessonUse the sensor you trust to teach the one you don't.

07

A Wi‑Fi scan every 34 seconds

The rear camera talks to the Pi over Wi‑Fi, and it kept vanishing for 15–25 s at a time, long enough to set off the "camera lost" alarm. A ping log every half second showed the Pi itself going deaf for 2–5 s, every 34 s. The culprit was our own status page: asking nmcli for the signal strength quietly started a full rescan whenever its list was older than 30 s, and a scanning radio leaves its channel.

Now the signal level comes straight from /proc/net/wireless and nothing rescans. When the camera does drop out, the service waits for its second stream to reconnect instead of restarting everything, and the picture is back in about 9 s.

LessonBefore blaming the network, check what you are asking of it.

08

Phantom knocks from a slipped FIFO

The alarm began reporting 1–3 g "impacts" with nobody near the van. The MPU‑6050 hands over its readings through a 1 KB FIFO of 12‑byte frames, under half a second at 200 Hz. A stray script pinning the CPU made the reader late, the FIFO overflowed, and the frames slipped by a few bytes: gravity suddenly appeared on another axis, a perfect fake jolt.

Now the reader checks the overflow flag before and after every read, rejects batches that are physically impossible or swing gravity by 50° in a blink, holds each batch back one read so a later fault can still cancel it, and resets the FIFO.

LessonA sensor's worst output isn't noise. It's confident garbage.

08 Built with AI agents

One lead, seven agents, then the road

fallout.nav was written by a team of Claude agents working in parallel. A lead agent wrote the plan and the contracts, gave each agent its own part of the repository, and then did the integration and all the work on the real device: a picture on the tube in about a day. Everything after that, the rear camera, the alarm, the radio, the games and remote access, came in about 180 commits over the next three weeks, driven by whatever broke or was missing in the van.

7agents in parallel, each in its own file zone
~1 dayfrom plan to a picture on the tube
1,112automated tests (794 Python + 318 JS)
~77klines of Python, JS and shell, almost a third of it tests

How it stayed sane

  1. Contracts first. Telemetry, REST API, settings schema and display components were fixed in writing before anyone started coding.
  2. One zone per agent. No two agents ever edit the same file; only the lead changes contracts.
  3. Mock everything. A simulated GPS track, compass and camera let all of it be built and tested on a PC while the Pi was still being flashed.
  4. One device, one driver. Deployment and on‑device tests are serial: the lead, or one agent at a time.
